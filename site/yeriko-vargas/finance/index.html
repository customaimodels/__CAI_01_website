# 🧬 LSTM STOCK PREDICTION: ADVANCED MATHEMATICAL & THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK 🧬
## *A 2026 Research Perspective on Recurrent Architectures for Non-Stationary Financial Time Series*

---

## I. FOUNDATIONAL PARADIGM: DEPARTING FROM EUCLIDEAN ASSUMPTIONS

### A. The Pathology of Classical Stationarity Tests

Traditional financial econometrics assumes **weak stationarity** (constant mean, variance, autocovariance):

$$\mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu, \quad \mathbb{Var}(X_t) = \sigma^2, \quad \text{Cov}(X_t, X_{t-k}) = \gamma(k)$$

**This is fundamentally false for equity returns.** Stock markets exhibit:

1. **Heteroskedastic volatility clustering** (GARCH effects)
2. **Stochastic volatility regimes** (vol of vol)
3. **Leverage effects** (negative returns increase future vol asymmetrically)
4. **Jumps and discontinuities** (gap risk)

**Solution:** Reformulate returns as elements of a **locally-stationary wavelet process**:

$$X_t = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} e^{i\omega t} A_t(\omega) dZ(\omega)$$

where $A_t(\omega)$ is **time-varying spectral amplitude** and $dZ(\omega)$ is orthogonal increment process.

**Why LSTMs exploit this:** The hidden state $h_t$ implicitly learns $A_t(\omega)$ through recurrent depth.

---

### B. The Curse of Regime-Dependent Dynamics

Equity returns follow a **piecewise stochastic process** with switching volatility regimes:

$$X_t = \mu_{S_t} + \sigma_{S_t} \epsilon_t, \quad S_t \in \{1, 2, ..., K\}$$

where $S_t$ is latent regime state following **hidden Markov chain**:

$$P(S_t = j | S_{t-1} = i) = \pi_{ij}$$

**Classical LSTM limitation:** Vanilla architecture cannot explicitly model regime switching.

**2026 Research Fix:** **Conditional Normalizing Flows + LSTM**

- Parametrize output distribution (not point estimates) via **invertible transformations**:

$$Z_t = f_{\theta}(X_t; h_t) \quad \text{with Jacobian} \quad J_t = \det\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial X_t}\right)$$

- LSTM generates **time-varying transformation parameters** $\theta_t$ that adapt to regime:

$$\theta_t = W_\theta h_t + b_\theta$$

- Model outputs: **mean, scale, skewness, excess kurtosis** (not just point prediction)

This converts point-estimation LSTM into **distributional forecaster** that learns regime-dependent densities.

## II. SPECTRAL DECOMPOSITION & FOURIER HARMONICS IN HIDDEN STATES

### A. Representation Learning as Harmonic Analysis

LSTMs with sufficient capacity learn Fourier-like decompositions implicitly:

$$\tilde{h}_t^{(l)} = \sum_{k=1}^{K_l} A_k^{(l)} \cos(\omega_k^{(l)} t + \phi_k^{(l)}) + \text{irregular}$$

The hidden state $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$ factorizes into:
- **Trend component:** Low-frequency ($\omega_1 \approx 0$, period $\gg 60$ days)
- **Business-cycle component:** Medium-frequency ($\omega_2 \approx 2\pi/252$, annual seasonality)
- **Intra-regime oscillations:** High-frequency noise

**Advanced implementation:**

Instead of standard LSTM cell, use **Wavelet-LSTM hybrid**:

$$\text{Cell}_{\text{hybrid}} = \text{LSTM}(x_t) + \sum_{j} \psi_j(t; \theta_j) \cdot \text{gate}_j(h_t)$$

where $\psi_j$ are **Morlet wavelets** (time-localized Fourier basis):

$$\psi_j(t) = \pi^{-1/4} e^{i\omega_j t} e^{-t^2/2} \quad \text{(continuous wavelet transform)}$$

**Advantage:** Captures **non-stationary periodicity** (e.g., earnings-cycle seasonality that SHIFTS over time)

---

### B. The Empirical Intrinsic Mode Decomposition (EIMD) Connection

Your LSTM's learned hidden states should approximate **Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD)**:

$$X_t = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \text{IMF}_j(t) + r_t$$

where **Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs)** are zero-mean oscillatory components with amplitude/frequency modulation.

**Mathematical bridge:** LSTM cell gate $\sigma(\cdot)$ creates **multiplicative gating** that implements *enveloping* (amplitude modulation):

$$\tilde{h}_t = \underbrace{i_t}_{\text{input gate}} \odot \underbrace{\tilde{c}_t}_{\text{cell state}} = \underbrace{A(t)}_{\text{envelope}} \odot \underbrace{\cos(\phi(t))}_{\text{oscillation}}$$

**Interpretation:** Your LSTM learns IMFs automatically through gate mechanisms.

## III. VOLATILITY FORECASTING: FROM GARCH TO STOCHASTIC VOLATILITY NEURAL NETWORKS

### A. Beyond GARCH: Continuous Stochastic Volatility

Classical **GARCH(1,1)** models volatility as:

$$\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2$$

**Limitation:** Single-parameter updating is too rigid for regime-switching.

**Advanced approach:** **Stochastic Volatility with Jumps (SVJ)**

$$d\ln S_t = (\mu - \lambda \kappa) dt + \sqrt{v_t} dW_t^S + (J-1) dN_t$$
$$dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t) dt + \sigma_v \sqrt{v_t} dW_t^V$$

where:
- $v_t$ is **latent volatility** following Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (mean-reversion)
- $N_t$ is **jump Poisson process** (captures earnings surprises, circuit breakers)
- $dW_t^S$ and $dW_t^V$ are **correlated Brownian motions** (leverage effect: $\rho < 0$)

**Neural representation:**

Use **Latent SDE framework** (neural SDEs):

$$d\mathbf{z}_t = f_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t) dt + g_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t) d\mathbf{W}_t$$

where LSTM generates **drift $f_\theta$** and **diffusion $g_\theta$** coefficients dynamically:

$$f_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t) = W_f \text{LSTM}(\mathbf{z}_t) + b_f$$
$$g_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t) = \text{softplus}(W_g \text{LSTM}(\mathbf{z}_t) + b_g)$$

**Output:** Distributional forecast with **time-varying skewness and excess kurtosis**, not just mean/variance.

---

### B. Realized Volatility & Jump Detection via Quadratic Variation

Modern volatility forecasting uses **realized measures** from high-frequency data:

$$\text{RV}_t = \sum_{j=1}^{N_t} r_{t,j}^2 \quad \text{(realized variance)}$$

$$\text{BV}_t = \frac{\pi}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{N_t-1} |r_{t,j}| \cdot |r_{t,j+1}| \quad \text{(bipower variation, robust to jumps)}$$

**Jump component:**

$$\text{Jump}_t = \max\{0, \text{RV}_t - c \cdot \text{BV}_t\}, \quad c \approx \Phi^{-1}(0.999) = 3.09$$

**Integration into LSTM:**

Augment input with **jump-sign indicator**:

$$\text{input}_t = [\log r_t, \log \text{BV}_t, \mathbb{1}(\text{Jump}_t > \tau), \text{vol\_regime}_t]$$

This forces LSTM to **explicitly learn jump dynamics** (rare large moves from diffusive noise).

## IV. INFORMATION GEOMETRY & FISHER-RICHTLER DIVERGENCE

### A. Geodesic Distance in Information Space

When training multiple models (ensemble), don't average predictions naively.

Instead, use **Wasserstein distance** between predicted distributions:

$$W_p(P, Q) = \left(\inf_{\gamma} \int \|x - y\|^p d\gamma(x,y)\right)^{1/p}$$

**Application:** Model 1 predicts $\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1)$, Model 2 predicts $\mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2)$

Closed-form Wasserstein for Gaussians:

$$W_2(\mathcal{N}_1, \mathcal{N}_2)^2 = \|\mu_1 - \mu_2\|^2 + \left(\sigma_1 + \sigma_2 - 2\sqrt{\sigma_1 \sigma_2}\right)$$

**Ensemble logic:**
- If $W_2 < \tau_{\text{low}}$: Models agree → high confidence
- If $W_2 > \tau_{\text{high}}$: Models disagree → low confidence (hedge)

**Alternative: Fisher Information Matrix**

Compute **parameter uncertainty** via Hessian:

$$H = \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta^2}$$

The **effective dimension** of learned features:

$$d_{\text{eff}} = \text{tr}(H^{-1} H_{\text{fisher}})$$

High $d_{\text{eff}}$ = model uses many orthogonal features (good generalization)
Low $d_{\text{eff}}$ = model relies on few features (overfit risk)

## V. COPULA LEARNING FOR STOCK CO-MOVEMENT

### A. Departing from Linear Correlation

Pearson correlation assumes:
$$\rho = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$$

**Problem:** Only captures linear dependence. Tail dependence (crashes) is **highly non-linear**.

**Solution: Copula Learning**

Sklar's theorem: Joint CDF decomposes:

$$F(x, y) = C(F_X(x), F_Y(y))$$

where **copula $C$** captures **pure dependence structure** independent of marginals.

**Archimedean copulas:**

Clayton copula (lower tail dependence):
$$C_\theta(u, v) = \left(u^{-\theta} + v^{-\theta} - 1\right)^{-1/\theta}$$

$\theta > 0$: Positive dependence strengthens in crashes (bad for diversification)

**Neural representation:**

Train LSTM to output **copula parameters** (not just mean/variance):

$$\hat{\theta}_t = f_{\text{LSTM}}([\text{vol}_t, \text{returns}_t, \text{correlation}_t])$$

This captures **time-varying tail dependence** crucial for portfolio risk:

When $\theta_t \uparrow$ (crashes approaching), all stocks co-move → diversification fails.

---

### B. Multivariate Student-t Copulas (Fat Tails)

Financial returns exhibit **excess kurtosis**. Better than Gaussian assumptions:

$$X_t | v_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma v_t), \quad v_t \sim \text{InvGamma}(\nu/2, \nu/2)$$

Marginal: $X_t \sim t_\nu(\Sigma)$ with **fat tails** ($\nu < \infty$)

**LSTM parametrizes:**
- $\mu_t$: conditional mean
- $\Sigma_t$: conditional covariance (via Cholesky decomposition)
- $\nu_t$: conditional degrees of freedom (kurtosis indicator)

$$\nu_t = \text{softplus}(W_\nu h_t + b_\nu) + 2.1$$

(Adding 2.1 ensures $\nu > 2$ for finite variance)

## VI. TEMPORAL POINT PROCESS THEORY & SELF-EXCITING JUMPS

### A. Hawkes Process for Clustering Events

Markets exhibit **self-exciting behavior:** Large moves beget more large moves.

**Hawkes process intensity:**

$$\lambda(t) = \mu + \int_0^t \alpha e^{-\beta(t-s)} dN(s)$$

where:
- $\mu$: baseline intensity
- $\alpha e^{-\beta(t-s)}$: **self-exciting kernel** (past jumps increase future jump probability)
- $N(t)$: cumulative count of jumps up to $t$

**Application to volatility regime:**

$$\text{vol}_t = \mathbb{E}[\lambda(t)] = \mu + \alpha \int_0^t e^{-\beta(t-s)} dN(s)$$

**Neural Hawkes LSTM:**

Combine Hawkes with LSTM:

$$h_t^{\text{new}} = \text{LSTM}(h_t) + \underbrace{\alpha_t e^{-\beta_t(t-t_{\text{lastjump}})}}_{\text{Hawkes self-excitement}}$$

where $\alpha_t, \beta_t$ are learned by LSTM.

**Interpretation:** Model learns how much to "remember" large past moves.
- High $\alpha_t$: Volatility persists (jump clustering)
- Low $\alpha_t$: Volatility mean-reverts quickly

---

### B. Marked Point Processes & Jump Size Distribution

Not all jumps are equal. Model **jump size distribution** conditional on jump occurrence:

$$P(\text{jump}_t \in [a, b] | \text{jump occurs}) = \text{mixture of distributions}$$

Use **neural mixture density networks (MDN)**:

$$p(y_t | h_t) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k(h_t) \mathcal{N}(y_t; \mu_k(h_t), \sigma_k(h_t))$$

LSTM outputs:
- **Mixture weights:** $\pi_k = \text{softmax}(W_\pi h_t)$
- **Component means:** $\mu_k = W_\mu^k h_t$
- **Component variances:** $\sigma_k = \text{softplus}(W_\sigma^k h_t)$

This creates **multimodal predictive distribution** capturing:
- Normal market moves (narrow Gaussian)
- Crash scenarios (wide left tail component)
- Euphoria scenarios (wide right tail component)

## VII. OPTIMAL STOPPING & SEQUENTIAL DECISION THEORY

### A. Bellman Equation for Portfolio Control

Frame stock prediction as **sequential decision problem** (reinforcement learning angle):

$$V_t(x_t, s_t) = \max_{a_t} \mathbb{E}[r(x_t, a_t) + \gamma V_{t+1}(x_{t+1}, s_{t+1})]$$

where:
- $x_t$: market state (prices, volatility)
- $a_t$: action (buy/hold/sell quantity)
- $s_t$: hidden regime state
- $\gamma$: discount factor

**Solution via LSTM value function approximation:**

$$V_t(x_t) \approx v_{\theta}(x_t, h_t)$$

where LSTM hidden state $h_t$ captures **sufficient statistics** of state distribution.

**Advantage:** Avoids myopic per-period predictions. Instead learns **multi-period optimal strategy**.

---

### B. Optimal Stopping Time (When to Sell)

Define **exit boundary** via **Free Boundary Problem**:

$$\max\{\underbrace{V_{\text{hold}}(S_t)}_{\text{value of holding}}, \underbrace{S_t - C}_{\text{sell now}}\}$$

where $C$ is transaction cost and $V_{\text{hold}}$ satisfies:

$$\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V'' + (r - q) S V' - r V + (r - q) S = 0$$

**LSTM learns boundary implicitly:**

$$\text{exit\_probability}_t = \sigma(W_e h_t + b_e)$$

When $\text{exit\_probability}_t > 0.7$ → Model predicts you should have already exited.

## VIII. KERNEL METHODS & FEATURE SPACE EMBEDDINGS

### A. Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) Perspective

LSTM hidden states can be viewed as **kernel features** in RKHS:

$$h_t = \Phi(x_t) \quad \text{where } \Phi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{H}$$

The kernel:

$$k(x_i, x_j) = \langle \Phi(x_i), \Phi(x_j) \rangle_\mathcal{H}$$

**Advanced:** Use **Mercer expansion** to decompose learned representations:

$$k(x_i, x_j) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \lambda_k \phi_k(x_i) \phi_k(x_j)$$

By examining **eigenvalues $\lambda_k$** of empirical Gram matrix:

$$\Lambda = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T h_t h_t^\top$$

High-variance directions (large $\lambda_k$) = important features
Low-variance directions = noise

**Dimension reduction:** Keep only directions with $\lambda_k > \epsilon$ threshold.

---

### B. Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) for Distribution Matching

When doing transfer learning (AAPL → COIN), ensure learned distributions are close:

$$\text{MMD}^2(P, Q) = \left\| \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[\Phi(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{y \sim Q}[\Phi(y)] \right\|_\mathcal{H}^2$$

Empirical MMD:

$$\widehat{\text{MMD}}^2 = \frac{1}{m^2} \sum_{i,j} k(x_i, x_j) + \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i,j} k(y_i, y_j) - \frac{2}{mn} \sum_{i,j} k(x_i, y_j)$$

**Training objective:** Minimize transfer loss + MMD penalty:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{prediction}}}_{\text{source task}} + \underbrace{\lambda \cdot \text{MMD}^2(h_t^{\text{source}}, h_t^{\text{target}})}_{\text{domain adaptation}}$$

This ensures transferred weights from AAPL/NVDA remain relevant on COIN.

## IX. VARIATIONAL INFERENCE & POSTERIOR UNCERTAINTY

### A. Variational Dropout as Bayesian Neural Networks

Standard dropout is actually **approximate Bayesian inference**:

$$\text{Dropout}(x) = x \odot z, \quad z_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$$

At test time, sample multiple dropout masks:

$$\hat{y}_1, \hat{y}_2, ..., \hat{y}_M \sim \text{model}_{\text{dropout}}(x) \text{ with dropout ON}$$

Posterior mean:
$$\mathbb{E}[y | x] \approx \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \hat{y}_m$$

Posterior variance (uncertainty):
$$\mathbb{V}[y | x] \approx \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \hat{y}_m^2 - \left(\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \hat{y}_m\right)^2$$

**Implementation:** At prediction time, NEVER disable dropout. Run $M=50$ forward passes.

This gives you **credible intervals** for predictions automatically.

---

### B. Variational Autoencoder (VAE) for Representation Learning

Use VAE to learn **generative model** of market states:

$$p(x) = \int p(x|z) p(z) dz$$

Encoder: $q_\phi(z | x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x), \sigma_\phi^2(x))$ (LSTM parametrizes $\mu, \sigma$)
Decoder: $p_\psi(x | z)$ reconstructs price from latent

ELBO (Evidence Lower Bound):

$$\mathcal{L} = -\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log p_\psi(x|z)] + \mathbb{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p(z))$$

**Application:**
- Latent $z$ captures **interpretable market regimes**
- Posterior uncertainty $\sigma_\phi(x)$ indicates how "normal" the market is
- When $\|\mu_\phi(x)\|$ is far from origin → Regime change detected

## X. CAUSAL INFERENCE & GRANGER CAUSALITY

### A. Granger Causality in High Dimensions

Does AAPL Granger-cause COIN? Test via **reduced vs full model**:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{reduced}} = \log \det(\Sigma_{\text{reduced}})$$
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{full}} = \log \det(\Sigma_{\text{full}})$$

**Granger causality test statistic:**

$$\text{GC} = T(\log \det(\Sigma_{\text{reduced}}) - \log \det(\Sigma_{\text{full}})) \sim \chi^2_k$$

**Neural adaptation:** Learn **attention weights** between stocks:

$$\text{attention}_{j \to i} = \frac{\exp(W_a [h_i; h_j])}{\sum_k \exp(W_a [h_i; h_k])}$$

High attention weight = strong Granger causality (AAPL → COIN).

---

### B. Instrumental Variables for Confounding

Problem: AAPL and COIN both rise → Is it causation or shared confounder?

Use **instrumental variable** (something that affects AAPL but not COIN directly):

Example: Semiconductor supply chain shock → Affects AAPL but COIN is just retail sentiment

Two-stage least squares (2SLS):

$$\text{Stage 1:} \hat{\text{AAPL}}_t = Z_t' \gamma + v_t$$
$$\text{Stage 2:} \text{COIN}_t = \hat{\text{AAPL}}_t \beta + \epsilon_t$$

**LSTM implementation:** Augment input with **exogenous instruments**:

$$h_t = \text{LSTM}([x_t, \underbrace{Z_t}_{\text{instruments}}])$$

Model learns to disentangle **causal effects** from **confounding**.

## XI. NEURAL ORDINARY DIFFERENTIAL EQUATIONS (Neural ODEs)

### A. Continuous-Time Dynamics via ODE Solvers

Instead of discrete LSTM cells, model continuous dynamics:

$$\frac{dh}{dt} = f_\theta(h, t)$$

Solution via ODE solver (e.g., RK4):

$$h(t_{i+1}) = h(t_i) + \int_{t_i}^{t_{i+1}} f_\theta(h(\tau), \tau) d\tau$$

**Advantages:**
- Memory efficient (no need to store all hidden states)
- Can evaluate at any time (not just integer timesteps)
- **Theoretical soundness:** Direct correspondence to continuous stochastic processes

**Implementation:**

$$f_\theta(h, t) = W_1 \tanh(W_2 h + b_2) + b_1$$

LSTM is now **learned differential equation**, not algebraic transformation.

---

### B. Neural SDE (Continuous Stochasticity)

Extend to stochastic dynamics:

$$dh_t = f_\theta(h_t, t) dt + g_\theta(h_t, t) dW_t$$

Use **Itô's lemma** for derivative calculations.

**Output:** Latent SDE parameters learned by neural network.

Stock price prediction becomes:

$$dS_t = \mu_{\text{learned}}(h_t) dt + \sigma_{\text{learned}}(h_t) dW_t$$

where $\mu_{\text{learned}}, \sigma_{\text{learned}}$ are functions of learned latent states.

## XII. INFORMATION BOTTLENECK & SUFFICIENT STATISTICS

### A. Information Bottleneck Principle

Given high-dimensional input $X$ (OHLCV data), compress to $Z$ (hidden state) while preserving information about target $Y$ (future price).

**Information bottleneck objective:**

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{IB}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} + \beta \cdot \mathbb{I}(Z; X)$$

where:
- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}$: prediction loss
- $\mathbb{I}(Z; X)$: mutual information between hidden state and input
- $\beta$: Lagrange multiplier (trades off compression vs accuracy)

As $\beta \uparrow$:
- Hidden state becomes simpler (fewer features)
- Generalization improves (less overfitting)
- But prediction loss might rise (can't fit training data as well)

**Sweet spot:** $\beta$ where test accuracy peaks → **Pareto frontier of compression-vs-prediction**.

---

### B. Sufficient Statistics via LSTM Bottleneck

Markov property: Future returns depend on past only through **sufficient statistic** $T(x_t, ..., x_{t-k})$.

For financial returns, sufficient statistics are:
- Recent log-returns (trend)
- Realized volatility (risk)
- Volume (information arrival rate)
- Regime indicator (bull/bear)

**Enforce sufficiency:** Add **information bottleneck loss** to LSTM training:

$$\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{(y_t - \hat{y}_t)^2}_{\text{MSE}} + \underbrace{\beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}(q(z|x) \| p(z))}_{\text{information regularization}}$$

This forces hidden state $z$ to discard irrelevant information (noise) while retaining predictive power.

## XIII. PERSISTENT HOMOLOGY & TOPOLOGICAL DATA ANALYSIS

### A. Persistence Diagrams for Trend Detection

Apply **persistent homology** to price data to extract **multi-scale structure**:

Create Vietoris-Rips complex from point cloud $\{p_1, ..., p_n\}$:

$$\text{VR}(\epsilon) = \{\text{simplices with diameter} \leq \epsilon\}$$

**Persistence diagram:** Plot births and deaths of topological features (connected components, loops, voids) as $\epsilon$ grows.

**Interpretation for stocks:**
- **Persistent 0-dimensional features** (clusters): Price consolidated in ranges
- **Persistent 1-dimensional features** (loops): Mean-reversion patterns
- **Ephemeral features** (short lifespan): Noise

**Integration into LSTM:**

Extract **topological features** from rolling windows:

$$\text{topo\_feature}_t = [\text{num\_clusters}, \text{num\_loops}, \text{avg\_persistence}]$$

Feed to LSTM as additional input channel.

---

### B. Mapper Algorithm for Regime Visualization

**Mapper** creates simplified topological representation:

1. Sample path $\{p_1, ..., p_T\}$ (closing prices over time)
2. Compute **lens function** $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ (e.g., $f = $ rolling volatility)
3. Cover range of $f$ with overlapping intervals
4. Cluster points in each interval
5. Create graph: nodes = clusters, edges = overlapping clusters

**Result:** Simplicial complex revealing **shape** of price manifold.

## XIV. KOLMOGOROV-SMIRNOV STATISTIC & DISTRIBUTIONAL FAIRNESS

### A. Model Distributional Calibration

Your LSTM outputs distributions $\hat{p}(y | x)$. Are they **calibrated**?

Test via KS statistic between predicted CDF and empirical CDF:

$$D_n = \max_y |\hat{F}(y) - \hat{F}_{\text{emp}}(y)|$$

where $\hat{F}$ is model-predicted CDF, $\hat{F}_{\text{emp}}$ is empirical.

**Hypothesis test:** $\sqrt{T} D_n \sim \text{KS}_\infty$ under null (calibration holds)

If $p\text{-value} < 0.05$: Model is **not calibrated** (stated uncertainty ≠ realized)

**Fix via isotonic regression:**

Learn monotonic transformation $m: \mathbb{R} \to [0, 1]$ such that:

$$m(\hat{p}(y | x)) \approx \mathbb{1}(y \leq y_{\text{realized}})$$

This **recalibrates** predicted probabilities to match empirical frequencies.

### B. Probability Integral Transform (PIT)

If $y_t$ is drawn from $\hat{p}(y | h_t)$, then:

$$u_t = \hat{F}(y_t | h_t) \sim \text{Uniform}[0, 1]$$

Plot histogram of $\{u_1, ..., u_T\}$:
- **Uniform histogram** → Good calibration
- **U-shaped histogram** → Model too confident (predictions too extreme)
- **Inverted-U histogram** → Model too uncertain (spread too wide)

## XV. METRIC LEARNING & CONTRASTIVE REPRESENTATIONS

### A. Siamese Networks for Stock Similarity

Learn **embedding** where similar stocks are close, dissimilar stocks are far:

$$\text{distance}(S_i, S_j) = \|f(x_i) - f(x_j)\|$$

where $f$ is shared encoder (LSTM).

**Training via contrastive loss:**

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{contrastive}} = \sum_{\text{pos}} \|f(x_i) - f(x_j)\|^2 + \sum_{\text{neg}} \max(0, \Delta - \|f(x_i) - f(x_k)\|)^2$$

**Positive pairs:** Stocks that co-move (AAPL & ASML in bull market)
**Negative pairs:** Stocks that diverge (AAPL & COIN when sentiment inverts)

**Advantage:** Automatically learns **stock similarity matrix** from data, reveals hidden sector structure.

---

### B. Triplet Loss & Hard Negative Mining

Instead of pairs, use **triplets** (anchor, positive, negative):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{triplet}} = \max(0, D(a, p) - D(a, n) + \alpha)$$

**Hard negative mining:** Actively choose hardest negatives (stocks most similar to anchor but different label).

This creates **maximally discriminative embeddings**.

## XVI. OPTIMAL TRANSPORT & WASSERSTEIN AUTOENCODERS

### A. Monge-Kantorovich Problem

Move probability mass from current state $P$ to ideal state $Q$ with minimum cost:

$$W(P, Q) = \inf_\pi \int \|x - y\| d\pi(x, y)$$

**Application:** Move predicted distribution toward true future distribution.

**Wasserstein Autoencoder (WAE):**

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_X[\|x - \hat{x}\|] + \lambda W(q_\phi(z), p(z))$$

where $W$ is Wasserstein distance, ensuring latent distribution matches prior.

**Benefit:** Smoother latent space (less interpolation artifacts) than VAE.

---

### B. Sliced Wasserstein Distance (Computational Tractability)

Computing Wasserstein in high dims is hard. Use **Radon transform**:

$$\text{SW}(P, Q)^2 = \int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}} W_1(\mathcal{R}_\theta P, \mathcal{R}_\theta Q)^2 d\theta$$

where $\mathcal{R}_\theta$ is projection onto random direction $\theta$.

Much faster to compute, still preserves geometric properties.

## XVII. ATTENTION MECHANISMS AS OPTIMAL TRANSPORT PLANS

### A. Self-Attention as Implicit Optimal Transport

Transformer attention can be reinterpreted as **optimal transport**:

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right) V$$

The softmax matrix is an **optimal coupling** (transport plan) between queries and keys.

**Interpretation:** For each time $t$, **allocate attention mass** to past timesteps that are most informative about future.

### B. Temporal Attention with Uncertainty

Parametrize attention **variance** (not just mean):

$$\mu_{\text{attention}} = \text{softmax}(QK^\top / \sqrt{d})$$
$$\sigma_{\text{attention}} = \text{softmax}(V_\sigma)$$

Output is **attention-weighted distribution** with **heteroskedastic uncertainty**:

$$y_t = \sum_s \mu_{\text{attention}}(s) y_s \quad \text{with} \quad \sigma_t^2 = \sum_s \sigma_{\text{attention}}(s) \sigma_s^2$$

This captures **how confident** the model is in its attention allocation.

## XVIII. ENTROPY & DIFFERENTIAL ENTROPY IN LEARNING

### A. Shannon Entropy for Feature Diversity

Compute empirical entropy of LSTM outputs:

$$H(h_t) = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i$$

where $p_i$ is probability of unit $i$ being "active" (high $|h_i|$).

**High entropy** → Units contribute equally (balanced learning)
**Low entropy** → Few units dominate (potential bottleneck)

**Objective:** Add entropy regularization:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}} = \alpha \sum_t H(h_t)$$

Forces network to use **all available capacity**, not just a few features.

---

### B. Differential Entropy for Continuous Distributions

If LSTM outputs continuous distribution (e.g., $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma)$):

$$h(p) = -\int p(x) \log p(x) dx$$

For Gaussian:
$$h(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma)) = \frac{1}{2} \log(2\pi e \sigma^2)$$

**Entropy as regularizer:**

High entropy (wide distribution) → Model is uncertain → Less likely to overfit
Low entropy (narrow distribution) → Model is confident → Risk of overconfidence

**Calibrated model** has entropy that **matches realized prediction error**.

## XX. META-LEARNING & FEW-SHOT TRANSFER

### A. Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML)

Learn **initialization weights** that can be fine-tuned quickly on new stocks.

**Outer loop (meta-train on many stocks):**
For each stock $i$:
1. Take gradient step on $\mathcal{L}_i$: $\theta' = \theta - \alpha \nabla \mathcal{L}_i(\theta)$
2. Evaluate on validation: $\mathcal{L}_i^{\text{val}}(\theta')$

**Meta-update:** $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \beta \nabla \mathcal{L}^{\text{meta}} = \beta \sum_i \nabla \mathcal{L}_i^{\text{val}}(\theta')$

**Result:** Initialization $\theta^*$ that, after 1-5 gradient steps on new stock, achieves near-optimal performance.

**Application:** New stock SSNLF arrives → Fine-tune pre-trained LSTM on 1 month of data → High accuracy

### B. Prototypical Networks

$$c_k = \frac{1}{|D_k|} \sum_{(x,y) \in D_k} f(x)$$

Classify new point by distance to nearest prototype:

**Benefit:** Clear **interpretable decision boundaries** in learned space.

## XXI. ADVERSARIAL TRAINING & ROBUSTNESS

### A. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) for Market Simulation

Train **generator** to produce realistic market paths:

$$G: \mathcal{Z} \to \mathcal{X}, \quad z \sim p(z), \quad G(z) \text{ looks like real price path}$$

Adversary **discriminator** learns to distinguish real from fake:

$$D: \mathcal{X} \to [0,1], \quad D(x) = P(\text{real} | x)$$

**Minimax objective:**

$$\min_G \max_D \mathbb{E}_x[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_z[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]$$

**Benefit:** Generate **realistic market scenarios** for testing trading strategy robustness.

**Defense:** Adversarial training on perturbed examples:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} = \max_\delta \mathcal{L}(x + \delta, y)$$

Train on worst-case perturbations → Robust to input noise.

**LSTM learns $H$ implicitly:** Recurrent depth captures long-range dependencies captured by $H > 0.5$.

**Numerical solution via Caputo fractional derivative:**

$$D_t^\alpha f(t) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(n - \alpha)} \int_0^t (t - s)^{n-\alpha-1} f^{(n)}(s) ds$$

LSTM can be modified to use fractional operations in gates.

A Harvard-Level Mathematical Treatment of Day-to-Day Stock Relationships

I. BEYOND LINEAR CORRELATION: THE GEOMETRY OF DEPENDENCE
A. The Fundamental Problem with Pearson $\rho$
Pearson correlation assumes bivariate normality:
$$\rho_{XY} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$$
Critical failure mode: Stocks with $\rho = 0$ (uncorrelated) can have strong nonlinear dependence.
Example: $$X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), \quad Y = X^2$$
Then $\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ (perfectly orthogonal) but $Y$ is completely determined by $X$.
Stock market analogy: AAPL and VIX have near-zero Pearson $\rho$, yet VIX controls regime in which AAPL moves.
Fix: Maximal Correlation (Hirschfeld-Gebelein)
$$\rho_{\max}(X, Y) = \sup_{f, g} \text{Corr}(f(X), g(Y))$$
subject to $\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \mathbb{E}[g(Y)] = 0$, $\mathbb{V}[f(X)] = \mathbb{V}[g(Y)] = 1$.
What this means: Find the best nonlinear transformations $f, g$ that maximize correlation. Even if $\rho = 0$, we might have $\rho_{\max} = 0.9$.
LSTM learns $f, g$ implicitly through hidden layers.

B. Distance Correlation: Capturing Independence Structure
$$\text{dCov}(X, Y) = \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[|X - X'| \cdot |Y - Y'|] - \mathbb{E}[|X - X'|] \cdot \mathbb{E}[|Y - Y'|]}$$
where primes denote independent copies.
Key property: $\text{dCov}(X, Y) = 0 \iff X \perp Y$ (truly independent)
Unlike Pearson, detects all types of dependence: nonlinear, nonmonotonic, extreme tail associations.
For stocks:
Day-to-day returns might have $\rho = 0.02$ (independent) but $\text{dCov} = 0.15$ (dependent).
Interpretation: Returns are uncorrelated but co-move under stress (hidden dependence).

II. COPULA STRUCTURES: THE HIDDEN CORRELATION SKELETON
A. Bivariate Copula Taxonomy
Clayton Copula (lower tail dependence): $$C_\theta(u, v) = (u^{-\theta} + v^{-\theta} - 1)^{-1/\theta}$$
Lower tail dependence coefficient: $$\lambda_L = \lim_{q \to 0^+} P(V \leq q | U \leq q) = 2^{-1/\theta}$$
Stocks with high $\lambda_L$: Co-crash in downturns (COIN, RBLX with VIX spikes)
Gumbel Copula (upper tail dependence): $$C_\theta(u, v) = \exp\left(-((-\ln u)^\theta + (-\ln v)^\theta)^{1/\theta}\right)$$
Upper tail dependence coefficient: $$\lambda_U = 2 - 2^{1/\theta}$$
Stocks with high $\lambda_U$: Co-rally in booms (NVDA, ASML in AI euphoria)
Student-t Copula (symmetric tail dependence): $$\lambda_L = \lambda_U = P(t_\nu > \sqrt{\frac{\nu+1}{1-\rho}(1+\rho)}) \quad \text{where } \nu, \rho \text{ are parameters}$$
Stocks with high symmetric $\lambda$: Same behavior up and down (tech sector, especially AAPL)

B. Conditional Copula: Time-Varying Dependence
Copula parameter $\theta_t$ changes with regime:
$$C_{\theta(h_t)}(u_t, v_t | h_t)$$
where $h_t$ is hidden state containing volatility regime, market direction, etc.
Extreme case: When $h_t$ indicates "market crash", $\theta_t \to \infty$ (extreme tail dependence).
LSTM learns: Mapping from market features $h_t \to$ copula parameter $\theta_t$.
Trading implication:
* When $\frac{d\theta_t}{dt} > 0$ (dependence increasing) → Diversification failing → Tighten stops
* When $\frac{d\theta_t}{dt} < 0$ → Diversification improving → Widen risk allocation

III. SPECTRAL ANALYSIS OF CORRELATION DYNAMICS
A. Eigenvalue Evolution of Correlation Matrix
Compute rolling correlation matrix $\Sigma_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ (where $d = 19$ stocks):
$$\Sigma_t = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_{\text{AAPL,AAPL}} & \sigma_{\text{AAPL,AMD}} & \cdots \ \sigma_{\text{AMD,AAPL}} & \sigma_{\text{AMD,AMD}} & \cdots \ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{pmatrix}$$
Eigenvalue decomposition:
$$\Sigma_t = \sum_{i=1}^d \lambda_i(t) v_i(t) v_i(t)^\top$$
where $\lambda_1(t) \geq \lambda_2(t) \geq ... \geq \lambda_d(t)$ are time-varying eigenvalues.
Interpretation:
* $\lambda_1(t)$ (market mode): "How much do ALL stocks move together?" When $\lambda_1 \uparrow$, systemic risk increases
* $\lambda_2(t), \lambda_3(t)$ (sector modes): Tech sector vs financials vs retail
* $\lambda_d(t)$ (idiosyncratic mode): Stock-specific risk uncorrelated with market
Marchenko-Pastur limit:
For random correlation matrix (null hypothesis: all returns independent):
$$\lambda_{\text{MP}}^{\pm} = \sigma^2 \left(1 \pm \sqrt{\frac{T}{N}}\right)^2$$
where $T$ = number of observations, $N$ = number of stocks, $\sigma^2$ = variance.
Test: If $\lambda_1(t) > \lambda_{\text{MP}}^+$ → Significant market-wide correlation (not random noise).

B. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) as Hidden Structure
First principal component explains maximum variance:
$$v_1 = \arg\max_{|v|=1} \mathbb{V}[v^\top X_t]$$
For your 19 stocks, typically $v_1 \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{19}}[1, 1, ..., 1]$ (equal weighting).
Interpretation: All stocks move together in primary mode (market beta).
Second principal component orthogonal to $v_1$:
$$v_2 = \arg\max_{|v|=1, v^\top v_1 = 0} \mathbb{V}[v^\top X_t]$$
Often aligns with sector split: Tech (NVDA, ASML, AMD) vs Retail (COIN, RBLX) vs Consumer (AAPL).
How LSTMs exploit this:
Hidden state $h_t^{(1)}$ learns $v_1$ (captures market mode) Hidden state $h_t^{(2)}$ learns $v_2$ (captures sector divergence) Hidden state $h_t^{(3)}$ learns $v_3$ (captures idiosyncratic moves)
This factorization makes learning efficient and interpretable.

IV. HIGHER-ORDER MOMENTS: COSKEWNESS & COKURTOSIS
A. Trivariate Coskewness
Beyond mean ($\rho$) and variance ($\sigma$), consider joint skewness:
$$\text{Coskew}(X, Y, Z) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{X - \mu_X}{\sigma_X} \cdot \frac{Y - \mu_Y}{\sigma_Y} \cdot \frac{Z - \mu_Z}{\sigma_Z}\right]$$
Example: When NVDA crashes (Z = NVDA return, large negative), do AAPL and AMD both collapse, or do they diverge?
If $\text{Coskew}(\text{AAPL, AMD, NVDA}) > 0$ → All three crash together (bad!) If $\text{Coskew}(\text{AAPL, AMD, NVDA}) < 0$ → AMD rallies when NVDA crashes (good for hedging!)

B. Quadratic Covariation for Jump Synchronization
In continuous-time framework (Itô calculus):
$$d[X_t, Y_t] = \text{Cov}(\sigma_X, \sigma_Y) dt + (\Delta X_t)(\Delta Y_t) \cdot \mathbb{1}_{\text{jump}}$$
First term: Diffusive co-movement (smooth) Second term: Jump co-movement (do both stocks gap together?)
Test for jump synchronization:
$$\text{Jump Corr} = \frac{\sum_{\text{jump times}} (\Delta X_i)(\Delta Y_i)}{\sqrt{\sum (\Delta X_i)^2 \sum (\Delta Y_i)^2}}$$
High jump correlation = gaps are synchronized = harder to hedge.
LSTM learns: Which pairs have synchronized jump risk (avoid these combinations).

V. DYNAMIC CONDITIONAL CORRELATION (DCC) & THRESHOLD BEHAVIOR
A. DCC-GARCH Framework
Bollerslev's DCC model separates volatility evolution from correlation evolution:
$$h_{ij,t} = \rho_{ij,t} \sqrt{h_{ii,t} h_{jj,t}}$$
where:
* $h_{ii,t}$ = univariate GARCH(1,1) for stock $i$
* $\rho_{ij,t}$ = time-varying correlation (evolves independently)
Dynamics of correlation:
$$\rho_{ij,t} = \frac{q_{ij,t}}{\sqrt{q_{ii,t} q_{jj,t}}}$$
where $q_{ij,t}$ evolves:
$$q_{ij,t} = (1 - \alpha - \beta) \bar{q}{ij} + \alpha \tilde{u}{i,t-1} \tilde{u}{j,t-1} + \beta q{ij,t-1}$$
Interpretation:
* $\alpha$ = how much recent shocks matter (high $\alpha$ = correlation reacts quickly)
* $\beta$ = persistence (high $\beta$ = correlation "locks in" after shock)
* $(1 - \alpha - \beta)$ = mean-reversion speed

B. Threshold Correlation Effects
Nonlinear DCC: Correlation depends on regime:
$$\alpha_t = \begin{cases} \alpha_{\text{high}} & \text{if } \sigma_t > \sigma_{\text{high}} \ \alpha_{\text{low}} & \text{if } \sigma_t < \sigma_{\text{low}} \end{cases}$$
Real behavior:
When $\sigma \uparrow$ (market stress), correlations become stickier (higher $\beta$, lower $\alpha$).
Market "freezes" in crisis: diversification evaporates, all correlations $\to 1$.
Threshold crossing:
Define critical volatility threshold $\sigma^*$ where correlation dynamics switch:
$$\mathbb{I}(\sigma_t > \sigma^*) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{crisis regime} \ 0 & \text{normal regime} \end{cases}$$
LSTM learns: Optimal $\sigma^*$ for each stock pair, AND learns how to predict threshold crossings.

VI. CORRELATION BREAKDOWN & CONTAGION
A. Forbes-Rigobon Adjusted Correlation
Raw correlation increases in crisis artificially (common variance channel).
Adjusted correlation removes variance effect:
$$\rho_{\text{adj}} = \frac{\rho - \rho_0 \omega_V}{1 - \rho_0 \omega_V}$$
where $\omega_V = \frac{\sigma_{\text{crisis}}^2}{\sigma_{\text{normal}}^2}$ is variance ratio.
Interpretation: Even after adjusting for higher volatility, do stocks still move together?
If yes: TRUE contagion (structural change in dependence) If no: Just "common volatility" (mechanical artifact)

B. Granger Causality Spectrum
Extend Granger causality to frequency domain (Geweke):
$$f_X \to Y(\omega) = \log\left(\frac{S_Y(\omega)}{S_Y(\omega) - |f_X \to Y(\omega)|^2}\right)$$
Interpretation at different frequencies:
* Low frequency ($\omega \approx 0$, long-term): AAPL Granger-causes COIN (institutional flows)
* High frequency ($\omega \to \pi$, intraday): COIN Granger-causes AAPL (retail order flow)
LSTM learns: Frequency-dependent causality structure (different relationships at different timescales).

VII. MULTIVARIATE EXTREME VALUE THEORY
A. Asymptotic Dependence in Tails
Standard correlation fails in tails. Instead, use coefficient of tail dependence:
$$\chi = \lim_{q \to 0^+} P(Y \leq q | X \leq q)$$
If $\chi = 0$: Asymptotically independent (no tail comovement) If $\chi > 0$: Asymptotically dependent (extreme moves synchronized)
Practical: AAPL and NVDA have $\chi \approx 0.4$ (40% chance of joint extreme move).

B. Generalized Pareto Distribution for Tail Modeling
Conditional on entering tail (exceeding threshold $u$):
$$P(X - u > x | X > u) \approx \begin{cases} \left(1 + \frac{\xi x}{\sigma}\right)^{-1/\xi} & \text{if } \xi \neq 0 \ \exp(-x/\sigma) & \text{if } \xi = 0 \end{cases}$$
Parameters:
* $\sigma$ = scale (tail volatility)
* $\xi$ = shape (tail heaviness, $\xi > 0$ = fat tails)
Stock-specific:
* AAPL: $\xi \approx 0.1$ (thin tails, bounded returns)
* COIN: $\xi \approx 0.3$ (fat tails, extreme moves likely)
LSTM learns: Different tail parameters per stock, AND learns when tails thicken (regime detection).

VIII. INFORMATION GEOMETRY OF CORRELATION MATRICES
A. Riemannian Manifold of Positive Definite Matrices
Correlation matrices live on Riemannian manifold (not Euclidean space):
$$\mathcal{P} = {\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} : \Sigma \succ 0, \mathbb{1}^\top \Sigma \mathbb{1} = d}$$
Proper metric (distance):
$$d(\Sigma_1, \Sigma_2) = \left|\log(\Sigma_1^{-1/2} \Sigma_2 \Sigma_1^{-1/2})\right|_F$$
This is affine-invariant (respects structure of positive-definite matrices).
Euclidean distance fails: Moving correlation from $[0.3, 0.5]$ is NOT the same as moving from $[0.8, 1.0]$.

B. Exponential Map for Prediction
Parametrize time-varying correlation via exponential map:
$$\Sigma_t = \text{Exp}{\Sigma{t-1}}(V_t)$$
where $V_t$ is tangent vector (in tangent space at $\Sigma_{t-1}$).
LSTM outputs: $V_t$ at each timestep.
This guarantees predicted correlation matrix is always positive-definite (no numerical artifacts).

IX. HIDDEN MARKOV CHAINS FOR REGIME-DEPENDENT CORRELATIONS
A. Viterbi Algorithm for Most-Likely State Sequence
Given observations (returns), find most likely hidden state sequence:
$$S_1^, S_2^, ..., S_T^* = \arg\max \log P(S_1, ..., S_T | X_1, ..., X_T)$$
Each hidden state $S_t \in {1, ..., K}$ has different correlation matrix:
$$\Sigma^{(1)}, \Sigma^{(2)}, ..., \Sigma^{(K)}$$
Example with 3 regimes:
* Regime 1 (Bull): $\rho_{\text{AAPL, COIN}} = 0.1$ (decoupled)
* Regime 2 (Normal): $\rho_{\text{AAPL, COIN}} = 0.5$ (moderate coupling)
* Regime 3 (Crash): $\rho_{\text{AAPL, COIN}} = 0.9$ (synchronized collapse)
LSTM learns: Implicit state transitions and regime-dependent correlations WITHOUT explicit HMM.

B. Baum-Welch for Parameter Estimation
Iteratively refine:
* Transition matrix: $P(S_t = j | S_{t-1} = i)$
* Emission distributions: $p(X_t | S_t = s)$
EM algorithm:
* E-step: Compute forward/backward probabilities
* M-step: Update parameters
Insight: LSTM gates ($\sigma, \text{tanh}$) implicitly perform this EM-like computation.

X. ABSTRACT VARIABILITY: VARIANCE OF VARIANCE (VOL OF VOL)
A. Stochastic Volatility of Volatility
Volatility is not constant. It evolves stochastically:
$$d\sigma_t^2 = \kappa(\theta - \sigma_t^2) dt + \gamma \sigma_t^{2\alpha} dW_t$$
where $\gamma$ is volatility-of-volatility parameter.
When $\gamma$ is large:
* Vol swings wildly (can spike from 10% to 30% overnight)
* Prediction is harder (even if mean direction correct, magnitude wrong)
When $\gamma$ is small:
* Vol stable (remains near long-term mean $\theta$)
* Prediction easier
LSTM learns: Latent $\gamma_t$ that adapts per stock/regime.

B. Variance Targeting & Correlation Instability
When volatility changes, observed correlations mechanically shift:
$$\rho_t = \frac{\text{Cov}_t(X, Y)}{\sigma_X(t) \sigma_Y(t)}$$
If $\sigma_X \uparrow$ while $\text{Cov}_t$ stays constant → $\rho_t \downarrow$ (spurious decorrelation).
Fix: Partial Correlation (controlling for volatility)
$$\rho_{\text{partial}} = \text{Corr}(X - \beta_X V, Y - \beta_Y V)$$
where $V$ is latent volatility factor, $\beta$'s are sensitivities.
This removes "pseudo-decorrelation" caused purely by vol changes.
LSTM learns: Partial correlations (true relationship) vs spurious correlations (vol-driven).

XI. CONCENTRATION & DIVERSIFICATION LIMITS
A. Frobenius Norm of Correlation Matrix
$$|\Sigma|F = \sqrt{\sum{i,j} \Sigma_{ij}^2}$$
Bounds:
* Minimum: $\sqrt{d}$ (completely independent)
* Maximum: $d$ (perfectly correlated)
Interpretation: $$|\Sigma|F^2 = d + 2 \sum{i < j} \rho_{ij}$$
When $|\Sigma|_F \to d$ (increases), diversification fails (correlations rising).
Market stress indicator: Monitor $|\Sigma_t|_F$ in real-time.
When $\frac{d|\Sigma_t|_F}{dt} > 0$ → Concentration increasing → De-risk.

B. Portfolio Variance Reduction via Diversification
Minimum variance portfolio weight:
$$w_{\min} = \frac{\Sigma^{-1} \mathbb{1}}{\mathbb{1}^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mathbb{1}}$$
Portfolio variance:
$$\sigma_p^2 = \frac{1}{\mathbb{1}^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mathbb{1}}$$
Diversification ratio:
$$\text{DR}_t = \frac{\sum_i w_i \sigma_i}{\sigma_p}$$
When $\text{DR}_t \to 1$ (diversification fails), correlation matrix $\Sigma$ is becoming rank-deficient.
LSTM predicts: When DR will collapse (before it happens).

XII. MATRIX PERTURBATION THEORY & STABILITY ANALYSIS
A. Spectral Perturbation: How do small changes in correlation propagate?
If true correlation is $\Sigma_0$ but we observe $\Sigma_0 + \Delta \Sigma$ (due to noise/estimation error):
First-order perturbation:
$$\delta \lambda_i = v_i^\top (\Delta \Sigma) v_i$$
Eigenvalue stability:
If two eigenvalues are close ($\lambda_1 \approx \lambda_2$), then small perturbations can swap them (unstable).
Stock implication: When tech and retail stocks are equally weighted ($\lambda_1 \approx \lambda_2$), small sentiment shifts cause dramatic portfolio rebalancing.

B. Condition Number: Numerical Sensitivity
$$\kappa(\Sigma) = \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}}$$
High condition number: Matrix is "nearly singular" (extreme leverage in inverse covariance matrix).
When $\kappa \to \infty$: Minimum variance portfolio weights explode (infinitesimal correlation change → huge weight change).
LSTM learns: When $\kappa$ is dangerously high → avoid aggressive tactical bets.

XIII. NONPARAMETRIC CORRELATION: SPEARMAN'S RHO & KENDALL'S TAU
A. Rank-Based Correlation
$$\rho_{\text{Spearman}} = \text{Corr}(R_X, R_Y)$$
where $R_X, R_Y$ are ranks (not raw values).
Advantage: Robust to outliers. COIN can have one 50% crash without distorting correlation.
$$\tau_{\text{Kendall}} = P(\text{concordant pairs}) - P(\text{discordant pairs})$$
where "concordant" = both increase/decrease together.
Interpretation: Kendall's $\tau$ connects directly to copulas (higher $\tau \Rightarrow$ higher tail dependence).

B. Resampling Correlation: Moving-Window Bootstrap
Estimate correlation confidence intervals via bootstrap:
$$\rho^{(b)} = \text{Corr}(X_{i_1}, Y_{i_1}, ..., X_{i_n}, Y_{i_n})$$
where $(i_1, ..., i_n)$ are sampled with replacement.
Distribution of $\rho^{(b)}$ over $B$ bootstrap samples gives you uncertainty.
When confidence intervals widen → Correlation estimates unreliable → Reduce position size.

XIV. DIFFERENTIAL CORRELATION EQUATIONS
A. Correlation as Dynamical System
Model correlation evolution as ODE:
$$\frac{d\rho_{ij}}{dt} = f(\rho_{ij}, \sigma_i, \sigma_j, \text{vol regime})$$
Simple linear version:
$$\frac{d\rho_{ij}}{dt} = -\alpha(\rho_{ij} - \bar{\rho}_{ij}) + \beta \Delta \sigma_t$$
where:
* $-\alpha(\rho_{ij} - \bar{\rho}_{ij})$ = mean reversion
* $\beta \Delta \sigma_t$ = shock from volatility change
LSTM solution: Instead of solving ODE, LSTM learns what $f$ is.

B. Lyapunov Stability Analysis
If correlation dynamics are stable (Lyapunov exponent $< 0$), correlation returns to equilibrium after shocks.
If unstable ($> 0$), shocks propagate and amplify (cascading failures).
Test via: $$\lambda_{\text{Lyapunov}} = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \log \left| \frac{\partial f}{\partial \rho} \right|$$
When $\lambda_{\text{Lyapunov}} \to 0^+$ (barely unstable) → Market at "edge of chaos" → Tiny shocks cause large moves.

XV. TENSOR METHODS FOR MULTI-WAY CORRELATIONS
A. Tucker Decomposition of Stock Correlations Over Time
Instead of 2D matrix $\Sigma_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, consider 3D tensor:
$$\mathcal{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d \times T}$$
where dimension 1 = Stock $i$, dimension 2 = Stock $j$, dimension 3 = Time $t$.
Tucker decomposition:
$$\mathcal{T} \approx \mathcal{C} \times_1 U^{(1)} \times_2 U^{(2)} \times_3 U^{(3)}$$
where $\mathcal{C}$ is core tensor (captures interactions), $U^{(k)}$ are factor matrices.
Interpretation:
* $U^{(1)}, U^{(2)}$ = spatial structure (which stocks cluster)
* $U^{(3)}$ = temporal evolution (how clusters change over time)
LSTM learns: Compressed representation via tensor factorization.

B. Correlation Prediction via Tensor Regression
Train model to predict future correlation matrix from past tensor:
$$\hat{\Sigma}{t+h} = G([\Sigma_t, \Sigma{t-1}, ..., \Sigma_{t-k}])$$
where $G$ is learned mapping (LSTM with tensor operations).

XVI. PHASE TRANSITION IN CORRELATION NETWORKS
A. Network Percolation Theory
Model stocks as network nodes, correlations as edge weights.
Define threshold $\rho_c$ (critical correlation) above which stocks are "connected".
Percolation threshold: At what $\rho_c$ does giant connected component emerge?
Phase transition:
* $\rho_c > \rho_{\text{critical}}$: Many small clusters (diversification works)
* $\rho_c < \rho_{\text{critical}}$: Single giant cluster (all stocks move together)
LSTM learns: When approaching phase transition (early warning of correlation breakdown).

B. Network Centrality: Which Stocks are "Hubs"?
Betweenness centrality: Stock $i$ is a hub if many correlation paths pass through it.
AAPL and NVDA have high centrality (moves in them affect everyone else).
LSTM learns: Dynamic centrality (which stocks are currently "contagion centers").

The LSTM layer and evaluation discipline
Your deep model should be one vote, not the whole constitution. PyTorch's nn.LSTM is a perfectly valid sequence model for this kind of work, but its value collapses if you evaluate it incorrectly. Scikit-learn's TimeSeriesSplit exists specifically because ordinary cross-validation would train on future data and test on past data, and the current API even supports a gap parameter to leave a buffer between train and test windows. Broader forecasting work has repeatedly argued for blocked or walk-forward validation in time series, and financial ML work continues to warn how easily look-ahead bias contaminates market backtests.

Given your screenshot's 5/10/15/30-day train/test structure and your goal of identifying the best buy/sell days during the current month, I would not ask the model to output one mystical exact date. I would train a pooled multi-horizon classifier that consumes the last 40–60 trading days of engineered features and outputs probabilities that the next 5, 10, 15, or 30 trading-day return will be positive and above a minimum hurdle. A pooled model is much safer than one tiny model per symbol, because three years of daily data yields only about 750 bars per ticker—far too small for ten separate deep nets to generalize well.

The sequence feature set should include at least returns, realized volatility, distance from 20/50-day means, support/resistance distance, volume ratio, relative strength versus SOXX or another benchmark, and peer-correlation context. If the model cannot improve the decision engine on those measures in honest walk-forward tests, it should lose voting power.
